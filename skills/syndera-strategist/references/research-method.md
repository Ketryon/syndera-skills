# Reading research the way a principal strategist would

Syndera's Intel research searches and reads the web and writes a brief where every claim points at its sources (`sources[]`, ids like `s3`). The brief is a starting point for judgement, not a verdict. Before you lean on any part of it:

## Is it sourced?

- A claim with no source id is inference. Say so, or drop it.
- Open the source when a decision hangs on it (a price, a launch, a quote) and the person can't easily check it themselves.
- Primary beats secondary: a competitor's own pricing page beats a blog post about it; a customer's review beats a listicle summarising reviews.

## Is it recent enough?

- Moves, prices, ads and campaigns go stale in weeks. Positioning and audience in months.
- Every move in the brief has a `when`. If it says "undated", treat it as old.
- `changes` says what moved since the previous brief: often the most useful part.

## How sure can you be?

- **High**: several independent sources agree, recent, and primary.
- **Medium**: one good primary source, or several secondary ones that agree.
- **Low**: one secondary source, old, or inferred from absence ("nobody talks about X" means research didn't find it, not that it isn't there).

Gaps carry a `confidence`; carry it into what you tell the person.

## What research can't see

- Competitors' own numbers (revenue, conversion, ad spend, follower growth) unless they publish them.
- Logged-in or paid places, private groups, email lists, and posts the networks don't show publicly.
- Ads outside the transparency libraries (most non-EU, non-political ads on X and TikTok).

Say what's not known instead of filling it in.

## Fair comparisons

- Compare like with like: the same kind of offer, the same audience, the same period.
- A competitor's weakness is only usable if customers say it (reviews, discussions), not if you infer it.
- Never suggest posts that name a competitor negatively unless the person asks and the claim is sourced and fair.
